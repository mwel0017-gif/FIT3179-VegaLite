1. Behind the Nutrition Label

What's Really Inside Packaged Foods?

Everyday foods can have very different nutrient profiles. In this aisle, we take a closer look at the nutritional content of everyday packaged foods, exploring how nutrients differ across food categories, how much of their sugar comes from added sugars, and how sodium levels vary across Australian packaged food products.

Aisle 01 · Shelf 1

The Nutrient Divide

How to read

Each cell shows the amount of a nutrient per 100 g of food. The colour indicates how high or low that value is compared to other foods for the same nutrient, rather than comparing different nutrients with each other.

Key insight

Nutritional strengths often come with trade-offs. Breakfast cereals lead in fibre (7.8 g) but also contain 20.1 g of sugar per 100 g. Snacks stand out for high sugar (32.1 g), while canned foods have the most sodium (813 mg). These contrasts reveal why understanding packaged foods requires looking beyond a single nutrient.

Aisle 01 · Shelf 2

The Hidden Sugar Story

How to read

Each bar shows average sugars per 100 g. Gold represents added sugars, while mint represents other sugars.

Key insight

Not all sugar comes from the same source. Sweet biscuits contain the most sugar (32.5 g per 100 g), largely from added sugars, while savoury sauces reveal how added sugar can hide in unexpected foods. Fruit juice contains mostly other sugars (10.2 g), whereas nearly all the sugar in soft drinks (7.3 g) is added. This highlights why the source of sugar matters just as much as the total amount.

Aisle 01 · Shelf 3

Salt in Unexpected Places

How to read

Each lollipop shows median sodium in milligrams per 100 g. Use the dropdown to highlight a food group.

Key insight

Sodium isn't just found in obviously salty foods. Commercial savoury sauces lead with 913 mg per 100 g, followed by cheese (602.5 mg) and potato crisps (591.5 mg). Surprisingly, bread and rolls contain more than twice the sodium of breakfast cereals (481 mg vs. 215 mg). This reveals how sodium can be present in everyday foods where we might least expect it.

2. The Health Star Puzzle

What Do the Stars Actually Reveal?

Health Star Ratings can help shoppers compare packaged foods, but ratings are not evenly distributed. Examine the overall ratings, the differences between products within a food type, and the shapes of rating distributions across categories.

Aisle 02 · Shelf 1

What the Stars Add Up To

How to read

Each star represents about 1% of the 246 rated products. Gold indicates 0.5–2.0 stars, teal 2.5–3.5, and mint 4.0–5.0.

Key insight

Lower-rated products make up the largest group, with 94 of 246 products (38.2%) receiving just 0.5–2 stars. Another 76 products (30.9%) fall in the middle range of 2.5–3.5 stars, while only 30.9% receive 4–5 stars. This means 69.1% of products (170 of 246), or about 7 in every 10, have a Health Star Rating below 4, showing that lower and middle ratings are more common than higher ratings in this dataset. These results are based on the 246 products analysed and may not represent all packaged foods sold in Australia.

Aisle 02 · Shelf 2

Same Shelf, Different Stars

How to read

Small dots are individual products. Gold marks the lowest rating and mint the highest rating in each category. The line shows the range.

Key insight

Even within the same food category, Health Star Ratings can differ dramatically. Breakfast cereals show the widest possible range, from 0.5 to 5 stars, while milk products range from 1 to 5 stars and snacks from 0.5 to 4 stars. Each dot represents a product, and the lines highlight the gap between the lowest and highest ratings. These differences reveal that products of the same type aren't necessarily nutritionally alike, making it important to compare Health Star Ratings even when choosing between similar foods.

Aisle 02 · Shelf 3

How Are the Stars Spread?

How to read

Each ridge represents the distribution of ratings within a food category. Compare the peaks, spread and category median.

Key insight

Even within the same food category, Health Star Ratings can tell very different stories. Breakfast cereals spread across nearly the entire rating scale, while vegetable products cluster towards higher ratings and snacks concentrate near the lowest. Milk products show distinct peaks at both lower and higher ratings, revealing considerable variation within one category. These patterns highlight that a food category alone cannot determine nutritional quality, and similar products may offer very different nutritional profiles.

3. Beyond the Stars

What Lies Behind a Single Score?

A Health Star Rating summarises a product, but its underlying nutrients tell a more detailed story. Compare five-nutrient profiles, investigate the sugar–fibre balance of cereals and snack bars, then examine four individual cereals with contrasting ratings.

Aisle 03 · Shelf 1

A Nutritional Fingerprint

How to read

Each line represents a packaged food product, showing its nutritional profile across five key nutrients. Values are scaled separately from 0–100% for each nutrient, allowing relative comparisons rather than actual nutrient percentages. Gold lines highlight lower-rated products, while teal lines represent other products. Use the food category filter to explore differences within specific groups.

Key insight

Lower-rated products often show higher relative levels of sugar, saturated fat or sodium, alongside lower fibre levels. However, nutritional profiles vary considerably, with some products sharing similar protein or sodium levels despite having different ratings. This highlights that a single Health Star Rating cannot fully capture a product's nutritional profile, making it valuable to examine multiple nutrients together.

Aisle 03 · Shelf 2

The Sugar and Fibre Balance

How to read

Each dot represents a packaged food product, with colour distinguishing breakfast cereals from snack bars. Products further to the right contain more sugar, while those higher up contain more fibre, both measured per 100 g. The dashed line marks the dataset's median fibre level of 7.6 g per 100 g, helping identify products with higher or lower fibre content.

Key insight

Breakfast cereals show considerable nutritional variation, with some containing less than 10 g of sugar and more than 15 g of fibre per 100 g. In contrast, many snack bars contain relatively high sugar levels while falling below the median fibre level of 7.6 g. These differences reveal that products within familiar food categories can offer very different nutritional benefits, highlighting the importance of considering both sugar and fibre rather than judging foods by their category alone.

Aisle 03 · Shelf 3

Behind the Breakfast Stars

How to read

Each radar chart shows the nutritional profile of a cereal across five key nutrients: sugar, saturated fat, sodium, protein and fibre. Values are scaled separately from 0–100% for each nutrient, with points further from the centre indicating higher relative levels. These percentages represent scaled values, not actual nutrient percentages, allowing comparisons of nutritional patterns across cereals.

Key insight

Even within breakfast cereals, nutritional profiles differ significantly. Weet-Bix (5.0 stars) stands out for its high relative fibre and protein levels, while Splitstix (0.5 stars) shows particularly high sugar and saturated fat with very little fibre. Corn Flakes (3.5 stars) stand out for sodium, whereas Coco Pops (2.0 stars) have relatively high sugar levels. These contrasts reveal that cereals in the same category can offer very different nutritional benefits and trade-offs, helping explain why their Health Star Ratings vary so widely.

4. Beyond the Supermarket Shelf

How Does Food Availability Vary Across Australia?

The nutrition story extends beyond individual packages. Explore geographic differences in estimated nutrient availability, sugar-sweetened beverages and selected packaged foods across Australia. These figures concern food availability, not what individuals actually eat.

Aisle 04 · Shelf 1

Inside the Regional Basket

How to read

Circles show estimated nutrient availability per 10,000 kJ. Gold marks metropolitan clusters and mint marks regional clusters. Hover over a circle to see regional nutrient details.

Key insight

Free sugar availability varies considerably across Australian regions, ranging from approximately 63.9 g to 92.8 g per 10,000 kJ. Some regional areas show higher levels than metropolitan areas, although this pattern is not consistent across the country. These differences highlight how the nutritional mix of packaged food purchases can vary by location, revealing regional patterns that national averages may overlook. The figures represent estimated sugar availability, not actual individual consumption.

Aisle 04 · Shelf 2

Australia's Sweet Drink Divide

How to read

The circle area shows estimated availability; the number in each circle is grams per 10,000 kJ. Tasmania has no published estimate in this chart.

Key insight

Sweet drink availability varies considerably across Australia. The Northern Territory records the highest published estimate at 234.8 g per 10,000 kJ, nearly 1.8 times the ACT's 128 g, while Queensland also shows a relatively high level at 191 g. These differences highlight significant regional variation in sweet drink availability, suggesting that the beverage landscape is not the same across Australia. These figures reflect estimated availability, not actual individual consumption.

Aisle 04 · Shelf 3

Beyond the Fresh Aisle

How to read

Mint indicates lower and gold higher estimated availability per 10,000 kJ. Uncoloured regions have no published value and should not be treated as having low availability.

Key insight

Packaged food availability varies across Australian regions, with the highest recorded level in Victoria (165.8 g per 10,000 kJ) and the lowest in New South Wales (134.4 g). This difference of 31.4 g highlights how the availability of selected packaged foods can differ considerably by location, even within the same state. The WA Outback and Tasmania have no published values, which indicates missing data rather than low availability. These findings reveal regional differences that national averages may overlook.

5. The Final Checkout

Are Health Star Ratings Reaching Shoppers?

Finish the journey by comparing reported use of Health Star Rating labels with the 70% uptake target.

Aisle 05 · Shelf 1

The Missing Stars

How to read

Each square represents one percentage point. The two grids compare reported Health Star Rating uptake with the 2025 target.

Key insight

Reported uptake is 39%, compared with the 70% target: a shortfall of 31 percentage points. That means 61 of every 100 products in the measured group did not display a rating. Health Star Ratings can support comparison, but nutrition labels remain essential when stars are missing.

Conclusion: What Did We Find at the Checkout?

Our journey through Australia's supermarket aisles reveals one important lesson: there is more to packaged food nutrition than a single number or star rating. From differences in sugar, sodium, fat, protein and fibre to the wide variation in Health Star Ratings among similar products, our findings show that foods within the same category are not always nutritionally alike.

Looking beyond the shelves, we also uncovered regional differences in packaged food availability, highlighting how Australia's food landscape varies by location. Yet, with reported Health Star Rating uptake at just 39%, Australia remains well short of its 70% target, limiting how consistently shoppers can use these ratings.

So, what's the final takeaway? Health Star Ratings offer a useful starting point, but they don't tell the whole story. Understanding what's in our food means looking beyond the stars, comparing similar products and reading the nutritional information behind every label. Because making informed choices starts with knowing what's really in our trolley.
